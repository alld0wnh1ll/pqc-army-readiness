1994 → Q-DAY · Army cryptographic readiness

The road to Q‑Day

Since August 2015, the government has been preparing for a quantum threat. This quantum threat is created by the potential of a future capable cryptographically relevant quantum computer (CRQC) having the capability of breaking modern asymmetric cryptographic suites (e.g., elliptic-curve cryptography (ECC) and RSA). Many security experts have estimated this threat to be as soon as 2030; whereas some estimate the threat much further out.

NSA and the Committee on National Security Systems (CNSS) have mandated programs to cease new investments into asymmetric public-key cryptography that protects command and control (C2) systems and prepares quantum-resistant algorithms. Origin Story · Finding the threat · 1994–2015

Petr Shor in 1994 showed a CRQC can break RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and ECC with relative ease. Grover soon after showed a quantum computer could cut symmetric key strength in half. Grover’s is a security concern, but less of one than Shor’s algorithm as we can double the key for symmetric encryption and get similar security guarantees as originally. If a CRQC existed, Shor's algorithm could break today's assymetric encryption in a day or less; a modern super comupter would take thousands of years or more to break the same encryption.

Hardware changes started chasing the math around 2001. Regev’s lattices (2005) are what today’s PQC standards stand on. Most NIST PQC standards rely on the assumption that quantum computers will have difficulty breaking cryptographic schemes based on lattice problems.

The field related to quantum computer resistant algorithms adopted the name “post-quantum cryptography” in 2006. The community identified many threats that a CRQC could pose, but the main one of the concerns at the time was attackers (nation states) storing large amounts of encrypted data and waiting for the day that they invent a CRQC which is also known as harvest now and decrypt it later (HNDL).The day this computer becomes available is known as Q-day. Map the network before PQC migration

Map the network first

An inventory is a live list of every place the old public-key crypto algorithms still run and still are used: apps, tactical radios, enterprise gateways, network devices (e.g., routers, switches, and firewalls), industrial controllers, and small devices. We begin building that list thoroughly now. We do not field PQC until we know what the network will do when keys get bigger and handshakes get slower. These unknown effects create an uncertainty gap. If we skip understanding the effects and rush the PQC migration, we will pay a second cost as we have seen with past migrations (once to field, and again to fix what we missed). Time spent now on inventory, latency, interoperability, and how Soldiers use the new equipment is cheap compared with a late re-fielding (tech refresh). The Army already paid for that lesson on WIN-T (tactical networking) and JRSS (enterprise security stacks). We are already taking proactive measures to ensure we find as many hidden requirements as possible, for it is absolutely necessary to migrate.

Spend the early money on requirements and discovery, not on a rushed PQC migration later. Finish this list first:

  • Find every place RSA and ECC still run
  • Map the apps and older networks that depend on that crypto
  • Set latency and throughput limits for bigger keys and slower handshakes
  • Include industrial control systems (ICS), operational technology (OT), and small devices
  • Account for tactical conditions and what operators can actually handle
  • Get vendor support and a way to swap algorithms later without replacing the box

Skip this list and the Army fields gear it has to rework, then does the job again with less time on the clock.

Start the inventory now. Waiting until a CRQC is close forces a rush.

Time runs left to right. The teal path is a PQC migration that starts with a thorough inventory: fewer surprises later, and a steady climb in cost. The red path waits until a CRQC looks close. Then the remaining requirements hit at once, the bill jumps, and the work can fail before it finishes. The more we requirements we find now, the less requirements that surprise us later. These curves only illustrate a concept. They are not based on measured data.

Lessons · WIN-T Inc 2

Field gear that formations can use

Warfighter Information Network-Tactical (WIN-T) Increment 2 put mobile tactical networking in the field at a scale the Army had not done before. Testing showed early nodes required too much on the operator for the system to function properly. Army leadership pivoted and redesigned the approach, which proved to be costly. For PQC, the operator needs to be a consideration as hard requirements from day one. Crypto solutions must be able to be implemented efficiently and effectively at every level of formation.

Lessons · JRSS

The hard problems show up when you move at scale

Joint Regional Security Stacks (JRSS) set out to put enterprise security on one modern stack after incidents on 9/11. The stack itself worked and provided a redundant, self-healing network for the Pentagon preventing single points of failure. The hard part showed up when trying to scale this system globally. The government ran into issues with old apps, older networks, ICS and OT paths, and extra delay (latency) on long-haul links. DISA closed those gaps as it assisted with the transition. PQC will hit the same issues because keys get bigger and handshakes get heavier and the underlying cryptographic schema, algorithms, protocols, and functions will change. We must find those paths now, while there is still time to write them as requirements. Every requirement we miss will leave a gap in the network, or have to be cleaned up during or after the migration, which adds scope to PQC migration projects.

Army PQC work is using those lessons now. Discovery and validation tools scan the network for the old crypto. New vendor products go into ATLAS before a Component (a Service or agency) considers a buy.

The network keeps getting tighter: more function in less gear, more apps on shared paths, and less slack for delay or extra complexity. A rushed PQC migration repeats two costly transitions the Army has already seen and experienced: gear formations cannot use in the field, and apps, older links, ICS, and latency that nobody mapped until the move was underway. Inventory is how those paths and requirements show up early.

04 · ATLASMaximize to readMinimize
DOW intake and tracking for PQC solutions

What ATLAS is

ATLAS means Acquisition, Testing, Licensing, and Security. It is the Department of War's gateway for PQC products (and later related work) before a Component (department nested in the DOW) looks at a buy. DOW has mandated that they are made aware of any PQC products being used or acquired. A government sponsor files the packet, ATLAS records intake and status, and Components will eventually see what is approved, how far it has gone (pre-test through fielding), national security system certification, and test results from other Components. This will prevent multiple Components from having to redundantly test products. However, it is important to ensure that the products another entity has tested fit the same characteristics as the environment it is going to be used on.

How it fits

Front door before Component review

New products come here first. ATLAS records the intake and tracks status. A Component still decides whether to test, buy, or field the product. ATLAS does not replace NIST standards, CNSA 2.0, or a program's own test.

Who submits

The government sponsor files the form

The government sponsor submits this questionnaire. The form collects common data points. The vendor receives the items in Question 13 and emails the accompanying documents. Incomplete packets close 7 days after the sponsor starts the submission.

What it tracks

Status and results across Components

ATLAS tracks approved solutions and what stage they are in. It shows national security system (NSS) certification status and performance-test outcomes from other Components. It is a shared map of what has been tried and how usable it was. It is not a blanket Department-wide approval to field.
